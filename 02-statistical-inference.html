Understanding Statistical Inference

Published

Nov 2026

  • ID: STATS-002
  • Type: Reference guide
  • Audience: Beginner to intermediate learners, researchers, and analysts
  • Theme: Explain estimates, uncertainty, hypotheses, and statistical evidence, including checks for AI-assisted interpretations

A sample gives us information about a larger population, but another sample would usually produce a different result. Statistical inference uses data and assumptions to estimate population quantities and assess uncertainty.

This chapter helps you distinguish the size of a finding from the strength of evidence against a statistical hypothesis.

1. Your question

What is the mean assessment score among learners in a training program, and how precisely can we estimate it?

We observe scores for a sample of learners. The sample mean is available to calculate; the population mean is the unknown quantity we want to estimate.

Term Meaning Training example
Population The group the question concerns All eligible learners in the program
Sample The units actually observed The learners whose scores we collect
Parameter A population quantity Population mean assessment score
Statistic A quantity calculated from a sample Sample mean assessment score
Estimate A statistic used to approximate a parameter Sample mean used to estimate population mean

Inference depends on how the sample was obtained. A narrow confidence interval cannot correct a biased sample or an inappropriate design.

2. Start with the estimate

An estimate answers “How much?” Examples include:

  • A mean score of 72 points.
  • A mean difference of 5 points between groups.
  • A pass proportion of 0.80, or 80%.
  • A difference in pass proportions of 0.10, or 10 percentage points.

State the direction and units. For a comparison defined as A minus B, a positive mean difference means A has the higher estimated mean.

An effect size describes the magnitude of a difference or relationship. It may be expressed in the original units, such as score points, or on a standardized scale. Original units often make practical meaning easier to judge.

3. Distinguish spread from precision

Standard deviation (SD) describes variation among individual observations. Standard error (SE) describes the sampling variability of an estimator under the assumed design and model.

For the mean of independent observations with a common finite variance, the usual estimated SE is:

\[ SE(\bar{x}) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \]

Here, \(s\) is the sample SD and \(n\) is the sample size. This simple formula is not appropriate for every design, such as unaccounted repeated measurements or clustering.

If the SD stays similar, collecting more independent observations generally reduces the SE. It does not necessarily reduce the variation among individuals.

More rows do not always mean more independent information

Ten measurements from each of ten learners are not equivalent to one measurement from each of 100 independent learners. The analysis must account for the relationship among measurements.

4. Understand a confidence interval

A confidence interval combines an estimate with uncertainty calculated using a specified procedure.

For a population mean, the usual one-sample t interval is:

\[ \bar{x} \pm t_{0.975,\,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \]

The multiplier is a t-distribution critical value for a two-sided 95% interval. Its suitability depends on the design and distributional conditions. The method chapters explain these conditions in context.

Worked example

Suppose a synthetic sample has:

Quantity Value
Independent learners 25
Sample mean 72 points
Sample SD 10 points
Estimated SE 2 points
t critical value, 24 degrees of freedom Approximately 2.064

The interval is:

\[ 72 \pm 2.064 \times 2 \approx [67.87,\;76.13] \]

We estimate the population mean as 72 points, with a 95% confidence interval from approximately 67.87 to 76.13 points.

The 95% describes the long-run coverage of the procedure: under its assumptions, intervals calculated from repeated samples would contain the fixed population mean about 95% of the time.

It does not mean that 95% of individual learners score within this interval. It also does not assign a 95% probability to the fixed population mean lying inside this particular observed interval.

Verify the calculation in R

This code uses summary statistics for illustration. It does not check the underlying scores or study design.

The following blocks display excerpts from the complete R script. Quarto does not execute them. Copy the script into scripts/R/ and run it from the repository root:

Rscript scripts/R/02-statistical-inference.R

The script uses base R and prints both the confidence interval and the test result. No additional R packages are required.

# Summary statistics for the synthetic example.
n <- 25
sample_mean <- 72
sample_sd <- 10

# Estimate precision and calculate the t-based interval.
standard_error <- sample_sd / sqrt(n)
critical_value <- qt(0.975, df = n - 1)
margin <- critical_value * standard_error

c(
  mean = sample_mean,
  standard_error = standard_error,
  lower_95 = sample_mean - margin,
  upper_95 = sample_mean + margin
)

Expected output, rounded: mean = 72; SE = 2; lower limit = 67.87; upper limit = 76.13.

The code is displayed for you to run in R; rendering this chapter does not execute it.

5. State the hypotheses

Suppose the reference mean score is 70 points. A two-sided test asks whether the population mean differs from that value.

  • Null hypothesis (\(H_0\)): The population mean equals 70 points.
  • Alternative hypothesis (\(H_1\)): The population mean differs from 70 points.

The hypotheses concern the population parameter. The observed sample mean need not equal the reference value even when the null hypothesis is true.

Choose a one-sided alternative only when it is justified by the question and specified before inspecting the results. Do not switch direction after seeing the data.

6. Interpret the p-value

A p-value is the probability, under the null hypothesis and the test’s other assumptions, of a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed statistic in the direction or directions specified by the test.

In the synthetic example:

\[ t = \frac{72 - 70}{2} = 1 \]

For a two-sided one-sample t-test with 24 degrees of freedom, the p-value is approximately 0.327.

# Run after the preceding summary-statistic code.
reference_mean <- 70

t_statistic <- (sample_mean - reference_mean) / standard_error
p_value <- 2 * pt(-abs(t_statistic), df = n - 1)

c(t = t_statistic, df = n - 1, p_value = p_value)

This result does not provide strong evidence against a population mean of 70 under the test assumptions. The 95% confidence interval also includes 70.

A p-value does not tell you… What to examine instead
The probability that the null hypothesis is true The test assumptions and broader evidence; hypothesis probabilities require a different inferential framework
The probability that the result occurred “by chance” The specific probability defined under the null model
How large or useful the effect is The estimate, units, uncertainty, and practical context
Whether the study design establishes causation Sampling, assignment, confounding, and other design features
Whether a large p-value proves no effect The interval and whether important effects remain compatible with the data and model

7. Understand thresholds and errors

A significance level, often written \(\alpha\), is a threshold specified for a testing procedure. A common choice is 0.05, but its appropriateness depends on the purpose and consequences of the decision.

Concept Meaning
Type I error Rejecting a true null hypothesis
Type II error Failing to reject a false null hypothesis
Power Probability of rejecting the null for a specified alternative, design, and test

For a valid test, the significance level controls the Type I error rate under its assumptions. It is not the probability that a particular reported finding is false.

A p-value of 0.049 and one of 0.051 provide very similar evidence. Avoid making sharply different scientific claims solely because they fall on different sides of a threshold.

For matching two-sided tests and confidence intervals constructed using the same method, a 95% interval excluding the null value corresponds to rejection at the 0.05 level. This correspondence need not hold when methods, adjustments, or alternatives differ.

8. Separate statistical and practical importance

Suppose a comparison estimates a mean score difference of 0.4 points, with a 95% confidence interval from 0.2 to 0.6 points. The interval excludes zero, but a difference this small may have little practical value.

Another comparison estimates 5 points, with an interval from -1 to 11 points. That interval includes zero and potentially important positive differences. The result is uncertain; it does not establish no difference.

Define a meaningful difference using the subject area, measurement scale, costs, and intended decision. Specify it before examining results where possible. Establishing equivalence requires a suitable design and analysis; an ordinary nonsignificant test is insufficient.

9. Use AI and check its suggestions

Example prompt

A synthetic sample of 25 independent learners has a mean score of 72 and a sample SD of 10. The reference population mean is 70. Explain the SE, a two-sided 95% t confidence interval, and the two-sided one-sample t-test p-value. State the assumptions that cannot be checked from these summaries. Distinguish uncertainty about the population mean from variation among learner scores. Do not describe the p-value as the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

Verify before using the explanation

  • Recalculate the SE: \(10 / \sqrt{25} = 2\).
  • Check the degrees of freedom: \(25 - 1 = 24\).
  • Check that the interval is centered on 72 and includes 70.
  • Check that the test statistic is 1 and the two-sided p-value is approximately 0.327.
  • Check that the interpretation addresses the population mean, not individual scores.
  • Ask which design and distributional assumptions remain unverified.

AI-generated precision is not evidence of correctness. Verify numbers and wording against the calculation and the method’s definition.

10. Report the finding

For this teaching example:

In the synthetic sample of 25 learners, the mean assessment score was 72 points (SD 10). The estimated population mean had a 95% t confidence interval of 67.87 to 76.13 points. A two-sided one-sample t-test against 70 points yielded \(t(24)=1.00\), \(p=0.327\). These data provide limited evidence against the reference mean under the test assumptions. The summary statistics alone do not allow assessment of sampling quality, independence, or unusual observations.

Keep the estimate and uncertainty central. Avoid reducing the report to “significant” or “not significant.”

11. Check your understanding

A report states:

The p-value was 0.18, so there is an 18% probability that no difference exists.

What is wrong with this interpretation? What additional results would you request?

Check your reasoning

A p-value is calculated assuming the null hypothesis and the test’s other assumptions. It does not give the probability that the null is true. Request the estimated difference, confidence interval, units, sample sizes, design, and relevant assumption checks. These help assess the magnitude, precision, and limitations of the finding.
