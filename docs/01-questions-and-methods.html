From Questions to Statistical Methods

Published

Nov 2026

  • ID: STATS-001
  • Type: Reference guide
  • Audience: Beginner to intermediate learners, researchers, and analysts
  • Theme: Match your question, outcome, and study design to an appropriate method, and evaluate AI-assisted suggestions

A statistical analysis begins with a question. Before choosing a test or asking AI for code, describe what you want to learn, what you measured, and how the observations were collected.

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to write an analysis question, identify your outcome and unit of observation, recognize paired data, and justify a starting method.

1. Your question

Suppose a training program offers two teaching approaches. You want to know:

How much does the mean assessment score differ between learners assigned to Approach A and learners assigned to Approach B?

This question identifies the outcome (assessment score), comparison (A versus B), and target estimate (the difference in population mean scores).

A useful question states:

Among [population], how does [outcome] differ or relate to [group or predictor], over [time period, if relevant]?

Avoid starting with “Which test gives a significant result?” Choose the question and analysis before inspecting which method produces the smallest p-value.

2. Describe your inputs

Record these details before selecting a method.

Detail Training example Why it matters
Population of interest Learners eligible for the program Defines whom the question concerns
Outcome Assessment score, from 0 to 100 A numerical outcome suggests different methods from a binary outcome
Group or predictor Teaching approach A or B Defines the comparison
Unit of observation One learner Identifies what each row represents
Independent unit One learner, if learners are assigned and assessed independently Determines the information available for inference
Design Different learners in each approach Distinguishes independent from paired measurements
Target estimate Mean score for A minus mean score for B Establishes the direction and units of the result

Outcome means the variable you want to explain or compare. A predictor is a variable used to describe or model variation in that outcome. Calling something a predictor does not establish that it causes the outcome.

Recognize the outcome type

Outcome type Examples Starting point
Numerical Height, sales value, assessment score Differences in means, numerical relationships, or other distribution summaries
Binary Passed/failed, purchased/did not purchase Proportions, risk differences, or binary outcome models
Categorical with several levels Transport mode, employment category Category frequencies and associations
Ordered categories Low/medium/high satisfaction Methods that respect ordering; scores need a justification
Counts or time to an event Number of visits, time until equipment failure Dedicated count or survival methods, beyond this guide’s introductory coverage

A number used as a label, such as a district code, remains categorical. Its numerical appearance does not make an average meaningful.

3. Check the study design

Independent groups

Different people, plots, or other units belong to each group. In the training example, each learner contributes one score and belongs to one approach.

Paired measurements

The same person is measured before and after training, or observations are explicitly matched. Analyze the within-pair differences for a paired mean comparison.

Two columns with the same number of rows are not automatically paired. Each pair must have a meaningful link.

Repeated or clustered observations

Examples include weekly scores from each learner, learners within classrooms, and several leaves measured from each plant. Measurements from the same learner, classroom, or plant may be correlated.

Do not treat every measurement as an independent replicate. A simple two-group test may be unsuitable; the analysis may require aggregation justified by the question, or a model that accounts for clustering or repeated measurements.

Independence comes from the design

A histogram or normality test cannot establish independence. Check how participants were sampled, assigned, measured, and grouped.

4. Choose a starting method

Use this table to locate a relevant chapter. It is a navigation aid; suitability still depends on the design, target estimate, and assumptions.

Your question Data and design Starting chapter
Does a population mean differ from a reference value? One numerical outcome, independent units One-sample t-test
How do two population means differ? Numerical outcome, two independent groups Independent-samples t-test, including Welch’s test
What is the mean within-pair change? Numerical outcome, paired observations Paired t-test
How do two independent distributions compare using ranks? Numerical or ordered outcome Mann–Whitney U test
Are paired differences centered around zero under a symmetric difference distribution? Paired numerical measurements Wilcoxon signed-rank test
Do several population means differ? Numerical outcome, independent groups One-way ANOVA and Welch’s ANOVA
Do several independent distributions differ using ranks? Numerical or ordered outcome Kruskal–Wallis test
Do measurements differ across repeated conditions? Repeated measurements on the same units Repeated-measures methods
Are two categorical variables associated? Counts in a contingency table, independent units Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test
How does a proportion compare with a reference or another group? Binary outcome, independent units Tests for proportions
Does a binary outcome change within pairs? Paired binary measurements McNemar’s test
How strong is a linear association? Two numerical variables Pearson correlation
How strong is a monotonic association using ranks? Two numerical or ordered variables Spearman correlation
How does a numerical outcome relate to one or more predictors? Numerical outcome, appropriate model structure Linear regression
How does a binary outcome relate to predictors? Binary outcome, appropriate model structure Logistic regression

Rank-based tests are not automatic substitutes for every failed assumption. Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis do not generally test only medians. Their interpretation depends on the distributions and assumptions discussed in their chapters.

For the independent training groups, Welch’s t-test is a reasonable starting point if the goal is a difference in means and the data support that analysis. Before running it, inspect the group sizes, missing scores, distributions, and influential observations.

5. Use AI and check its suggestions

Write your own question and input summary first. Then ask AI to explain a possible method and identify missing information.

Example prompt

I am comparing assessment scores between two teaching approaches. Each learner belongs to one approach and contributes one score from 0 to 100. My target estimate is the population mean score for A minus that for B. Explain whether Welch’s t-test is suitable. Ask about sampling, assignment, clustering, sample sizes, missing values, and unusual observations before recommending an analysis. State the assumptions, alternatives, and limits of the interpretation. Do not invent results.

Use synthetic examples or appropriately de-identified summaries when discussing data with an AI tool. Do not include identifying or confidential records without authorization.

Check the response

AI suggestion Your check
“Use a paired t-test.” Are these the same learners measured twice or genuinely matched pairs?
“Use a t-test because the outcome is numerical.” Does the design support independent units, and is a mean difference the intended estimate?
“The sample is large, so assumptions do not matter.” Are there clusters, influential observations, or sampling problems that sample size cannot fix?
“A significant difference proves the teaching approach works.” Does the design support a causal interpretation? Could selection or confounding explain the association?
“Use Mann–Whitney to test the medians.” What assumptions would allow a location or median interpretation?

AI can help draft code, explain output, and suggest checks. Run and inspect the code yourself. Verify documentation for unfamiliar functions and keep a record of consequential analysis decisions.

6. Write your analysis plan

For the training example, a short plan could read:

We will compare assessment scores between Approach A and Approach B using the difference in means, defined as A minus B. Each learner contributes one observation. We will examine group sizes, missing scores, score distributions, and unusual observations before analysis. If an independent mean comparison is appropriate, we will use Welch’s t-test and report the estimated mean difference, its 95% confidence interval, and the two-sided p-value. Interpretation will account for how learners were recruited and assigned to approaches.

Specify any adjustment variables, subgroup analyses, and multiple comparisons before examining their results. If the plan changes, record the reason and distinguish planned from exploratory work.

7. Verify and explain

Before proceeding, answer these questions in your own words:

  1. What is my outcome, and what type of variable is it?
  2. What does one row represent? What is the independent unit?
  3. Are observations independent, paired, repeated, or clustered?
  4. What quantity do I want to estimate, including its direction and units?
  5. Why does my proposed method fit that question and design?
  6. What could limit the interpretation, even if the p-value is small?

Try it

A farm records crop yield from 12 plots before and after a soil treatment. Each plot has a unique identifier and contributes both measurements.

  • Is this an independent or paired comparison?
  • What quantity would you estimate?
  • What limits a claim that the treatment caused any observed change?
Check your reasoning

The measurements are paired by plot. A useful target is the mean within-plot yield change, defined as after minus before and expressed in the yield units. A paired t-test may be appropriate if the plot differences meet its conditions and plots provide independent replication. Without an appropriate comparison group or design, weather, season, and other changes could explain the observed difference. Pairing alone does not establish causation.

8. Common mistakes

  • Selecting a test solely from the outcome type while ignoring the design.
  • Treating multiple measurements from one unit as independent replicates.
  • Choosing a method because it produces a smaller p-value.
  • Interpreting a small p-value as a large or practically important effect.
  • Accepting an AI explanation without checking the inputs, assumptions, and actual output.
