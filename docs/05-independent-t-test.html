Independent-Samples t-Test

Published

Nov 2026

  • ID: STATS-005
  • Type: Statistical method reference
  • Audience: Beginner to intermediate learners, researchers, and analysts
  • Theme: Compare two independent population means using Welch’s t-test and explain the difference, uncertainty, and limitations

1. Your question

How much does the population mean assessment score differ between teaching approaches A and B?

Our target is mean A minus mean B, in score points. A negative estimate means A has the lower mean; a positive estimate means A has the higher mean.

This example uses 40 synthetic learners, with 20 complete scores per group. The scores reuse the observed values from Chapter 03 for a standalone illustration. Excluding that chapter’s missing scores would require justification in an applied study.

2. When to use this method

Use an independent-samples t-test to compare the means of a numerical outcome in two independent groups.

Examples include yield from independently assigned field plots, expenditure among independent customers in two groups, and assessment scores from different learners.

Welch’s t-test estimates the difference without assuming equal population variances. It is a useful starting choice for independent mean comparisons. It still requires a suitable design and attention to the outcome distributions.

Design Relevant approach
Different independent learners in A and B Independent-samples t-test
Same learners before and after training Paired t-test
Learners grouped within classrooms A method accounting for classroom clustering
More than two independent groups One-way ANOVA methods
Numerical outcome with important adjustment variables Linear regression, with appropriate design and diagnostics

Equal group sizes do not make observations paired. Meaningful pairing must come from the design.

3. What it estimates or tests

  • Target: \(\mu_A-\mu_B\), the population mean difference.
  • Null hypothesis: \(H_0: \mu_A-\mu_B=0\).
  • Two-sided alternative: \(H_1: \mu_A-\mu_B\ne0\).

Welch’s estimated standard error is:

\[ SE = \sqrt{\frac{s_A^2}{n_A}+\frac{s_B^2}{n_B}}. \]

The test statistic is:

\[ t = \frac{\bar{x}_A-\bar{x}_B}{SE}. \]

The confidence interval is the estimated difference plus or minus a t critical value multiplied by this SE. Welch’s method uses approximate degrees of freedom that can be fractional.

Optional: Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom

\[ \nu = \frac{(s_A^2/n_A+s_B^2/n_B)^2} {(s_A^2/n_A)^2/(n_A-1)+(s_B^2/n_B)^2/(n_B-1)}. \]

This accounts for uncertainty in the two variance estimates. Unlike the pooled equal-variance test, the degrees of freedom need not equal \(n_A+n_B-2\).

4. Check your data and assumptions

Before testing, verify:

  • Each learner contributes one score and belongs to one group.
  • Observations are independent within and between groups; classroom or household dependence is addressed.
  • Scores have consistent units and a meaningful numerical scale.
  • Missing scores, duplicate records, and exclusions have been investigated.
  • Group distributions do not show severe skewness or influential extremes that undermine small-sample inference.
  • Sampling and assignment support the population and interpretation of interest.

Inspect individual scores and Q-Q plots within each group. The group SDs are approximately 6.97 and 13.96 points, so equal variability should not be assumed. Welch’s method addresses unequal variances, but does not correct dependence, selection bias, or confounding.

Welch versus pooled Student’s t-test

The pooled test assumes equal population variances and uses a pooled variance estimate. Welch’s test allows unequal variances. Do not automatically select between them using a preliminary variance-test p-value. Choose an approach based on the design and a justified analysis plan.

A normality-test p-value alone should not decide whether to use a mean comparison or a rank-based method. They can address different inferential targets.

5. Run the analysis

Copy the companion files into the repository root:

  • Synthetic input
  • Complete base R script
Rscript scripts/R/05-independent-t-test.R

The displayed blocks below are plain, non-executable code. Quarto does not run them. The complete script checks the input and exports results and figures.

# Explicitly select each group to preserve the direction A minus B.
dat <- read.csv("data/example/05-two-group-scores.csv",
                stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
a <- dat$score[dat$approach == "A"]
b <- dat$score[dat$approach == "B"]

fit <- t.test(a, b, alternative = "two.sided", var.equal = FALSE,
              conf.level = 0.95)
print(fit)
# The interval returned here is already for mean A minus mean B.
c(difference = mean(a) - mean(b),
  lower_95 = fit$conf.int[1],
  upper_95 = fit$conf.int[2])

6. Verify and explain the output

Expected results, rounded:

Quantity Value
Learners per group 20
Mean A 69.30 points
Mean B 74.10 points
SD A 6.97 points
SD B 13.96 points
Mean difference, A minus B -4.80 points
Standard error of difference 3.488 points
95% CI for difference -11.95 to 2.35 points
Welch t statistic -1.376
Approximate degrees of freedom 27.91
Two-sided p-value 0.1798

Check the arithmetic: \(69.30-74.10=-4.80\), and \(-4.80/3.488\approx-1.376\).

The estimated mean for A is 4.80 points lower than for B. The interval includes zero and extends from an appreciably lower mean for A to a slightly higher mean. The result does not establish equality, equivalence, or absence of an important difference.

The p-value is calculated under the equal-means null and the test’s other assumptions. It is not the probability that the two population means are equal.

Read the generated figures

In the diagnostic figure, inspect spread and unusual scores within each group. A straight Q-Q pattern does not establish independence.

In the difference figure, the dot represents -4.80, the line represents the 95% interval, and the dashed zero reference marks equal population means. This interval describes uncertainty about a mean difference, not the range of differences between individual learners.

Reversing the comparison to B minus A changes the signs: estimate +4.80, interval -2.35 to 11.95, and positive t statistic. The two-sided p-value stays the same.

7. Use AI and check its suggestions

I want to compare assessment scores from two independent groups, with 20 learners per group. Define the difference as A minus B. Mean scores are 69.30 and 74.10; SDs are approximately 6.97 and 13.96. Explain Welch’s t-test, identify missing design information, and distinguish a nonsignificant result from evidence of equivalence. Use the supplied CSV for exact calculations. Do not reverse the comparison or claim that an approach caused the difference.

Check that the response uses var.equal = FALSE, preserves A minus B, reports a negative estimate, allows fractional degrees of freedom, and interprets the interval alongside the p-value.

If it says “the approaches are equally effective,” ask what equivalence margin and study design support that conclusion. An ordinary test failing to reject equal means is insufficient.

8. Report your finding

In the synthetic example, mean scores were 69.30 points (SD 6.97, n=20) for A and 74.10 points (SD 13.96, n=20) for B. The estimated difference, A minus B, was -4.80 points (95% CI -11.95 to 2.35). A two-sided Welch t-test yielded \(t(27.91)=-1.38\), \(p=0.1798\). The interval includes zero and potentially important differences; these results do not establish equivalence. A causal interpretation would require appropriate assignment and other design conditions.

An applied report should also describe sampling, missing-data handling, diagnostics, and any deviations from the planned analysis.

9. Common mistakes and alternatives

Mistake Better approach
Using a paired test because group sizes match Identify whether observations are genuinely linked
Ignoring clustering within classrooms Account for the correlated structure
Changing the comparison direction midway State A minus B consistently in code and reporting
Assuming p > 0.05 means equality Examine precision and important differences; use equivalence methods when appropriate
Inferring causation from a group comparison alone Assess assignment, confounding, and the broader design
Searching for a test with a smaller p-value Preserve the inferential target and document justified changes

Severe skewness, influential observations, or a different target quantity may motivate another approach. Mann–Whitney is a rank-based comparison and does not generally test arithmetic means or only medians. Transformations change the scale of inference. Regression may support adjustment for prespecified covariates, but adjustment alone does not establish causation. Review the relevant method’s assumptions before switching.

10. Check your understanding

An analyst reports a mean difference A minus B of -4.80 points, with a 95% interval from -11.95 to 2.35, then writes: “B is certainly better, but the test had no effect because p > 0.05.”

Which parts need correction?

Check your reasoning

B has the higher sample mean, but uncertainty includes zero and a small difference in the opposite direction. The interval also includes potentially important differences favoring B. A large p-value does not establish no effect. “Better” additionally requires a meaningful outcome, practical context, and a design that supports the intended claim.
