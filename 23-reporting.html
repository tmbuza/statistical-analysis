Reporting Statistical Findings

Published

Nov 2026

  • ID: STATS-023
  • Type: Reporting reference
  • Audience: Beginner to intermediate learners, researchers, and analysts
  • Theme: Present estimates, uncertainty, methods, and limitations in a clear account that readers can trace to the analysis

1. Your task

Explain what the analysis found, how it was obtained, and what it does not establish.

A statistical report should answer the original question. Readers need the size and direction of the finding, its uncertainty, the relevant design and methods, and the limits of interpretation.

This chapter reuses Chapter 05’s synthetic independent-group scores to demonstrate a reproducible reporting workflow. It introduces no new statistical test.

2. Start with an evidence summary

Before writing, assemble the information needed to support the claim.

Element What to record
Question Population, outcome, comparison, and target quantity
Design Independent, paired, repeated, clustered, or another structure
Data Source, units, sample sizes, exclusions, and missingness
Method Test or model, alternatives, reference categories, and key assumptions
Estimate Magnitude, direction, and meaningful units
Uncertainty Interval level and construction; test result when relevant
Diagnostics Checks performed, findings, and consequential problems
Multiplicity Family, adjustment, and planned versus exploratory analyses
Limitations Bias, confounding, imprecision, generalizability, and design restrictions
Reproducibility Input version, script, software environment, and output paths

Do not invent missing design details to make a paragraph sound complete. Mark unresolved facts and investigate them before applied reporting.

3. Use a short results structure

A useful sequence is:

  1. Describe the analyzed sample and relevant group summaries.
  2. State the estimate and its direction in units the reader understands.
  3. Report an appropriate interval and, where relevant, the test statistic and p-value.
  4. Answer the question with uncertainty and design limitations.

A general writing template is:

Among [analyzed units], [descriptive summary]. The estimated [target quantity], defined as [direction], was [estimate and units] ([interval level and method] CI [limits]). [Specified test or model] yielded [statistic, degrees of freedom if applicable, p-value and adjustment status]. These results [careful answer], with limitations from [relevant issues].

Adapt the template to the actual method. An exact test may have no familiar t statistic or degrees of freedom to report. Do not force every analysis into identical fields.

4. Worked reporting example

Quantity Synthetic result
Group A n=20; mean 69.30; SD 6.97
Group B n=20; mean 74.10; SD 13.96
Mean difference, A minus B -4.80 points
95% Welch confidence interval -11.95 to 2.35 points
Welch t statistic -1.376
Approximate df 27.91
Two-sided p-value 0.1798

A suitable results paragraph is:

In the synthetic example, mean assessment scores were 69.30 points (SD 6.97, n=20) in A and 74.10 points (SD 13.96, n=20) in B. The estimated mean difference, A minus B, was -4.80 points (95% Welch CI -11.95 to 2.35). A two-sided Welch t-test gave t(27.91)=-1.38, p=0.1798. The interval includes zero and potentially important differences; these results do not establish equivalence or a causal group effect.

For a less technical audience:

The sample mean was 4.8 points lower in A than in B. The uncertainty interval ranged from about 12 points lower to 2.3 points higher. The data do not give a precise answer about the population difference, and this comparison alone does not show that either approach caused a change in scores.

These paragraphs summarize a teaching calculation. A real report must include the actual sampling process, diagnostic findings, missing-data handling, and relevant study context.

5. Separate methods, results, and interpretation

Section Purpose Example content
Methods Explain what was planned and done Independent-unit design, outcome definition, Welch test, interval level, missingness strategy
Results Present observed summaries and estimates Group means, A-minus-B difference, interval, and p-value
Discussion Explain meaning and limitations Practical importance, uncertainty, possible bias, and scope of claims

Record departures from the plan and why they occurred. Label exploratory findings and sensitivity analyses. Do not rewrite the methods as if an outcome-driven decision had been prespecified.

6. Report numbers consistently

Use enough precision to support interpretation without implying unrealistic accuracy. Keep units and comparison direction consistent across text, tables, code, and figures.

  • Report an SD for individual variation and an SE or interval for estimate uncertainty; do not interchange them.
  • State whether an interval is ordinary, simultaneous, bootstrap, Wald, exact conditional, or another construction.
  • Label adjusted p-values with the method and comparison family.
  • Avoid p=0.000. Use suitable scientific notation or a clearly stated reporting threshold.
  • Distinguish percentage points from relative percentage changes.
  • Distinguish odds ratios, proportion ratios, and absolute differences.

A 95% confidence interval describes the long-run performance of its procedure under assumptions. It does not generally assign a 95% probability to the fixed parameter lying within the observed interval.

7. Choose language that matches the evidence

Avoid More defensible wording
“There was no difference” solely because p > 0.05 “The interval includes zero and differences that may matter; the estimate is uncertain”
“The result proves the intervention works” “The result supports [specific claim] under the design and analysis assumptions”
“X causes Y” from an observational association “X was associated with Y,” with potential confounding explained
“The result was significant,” with no magnitude Report the estimate, interval, and practical context
“The groups are equivalent” after a nonsignificant ordinary test Explain that equivalence needs a justified margin and appropriate procedure
“AI verified the result” Describe the checks actually performed by the analyst and software

A small p-value does not establish a large or useful difference. A large p-value does not establish absence. Keep the estimate, uncertainty, and decision context together.

8. Make figures and tables interpretable

Use figures when they clarify distributions, relationships, or uncertainty. Show individual observations where suitable, preserve pairing visually when relevant, and state units and denominators.

For interval plots, explain what the dot and interval represent. Distinguish mean confidence intervals from individual prediction intervals. Avoid significance stars as the only explanation of evidence.

A table should identify the sample or denominator, estimate, direction, interval construction, and adjustment status. State whether descriptive summaries use all available observations or the same analysis sample as the inferential result.

9. Reproduce the reporting example

Copy these companion files into the repository root:

  • Synthetic input
  • Complete base R script
  • Reusable reporting template
Rscript scripts/R/23-reporting.R

All code blocks are non-executable displays. The actual calculations and exports are in the script.

# Recalculate the numeric evidence rather than copying rounded values by hand.
fit <- t.test(a, b, var.equal = FALSE, alternative = "two.sided",
              conf.level = 0.95)
mean(a) - mean(b)
fit$conf.int
fit$p.value

The full script reads and checks the input before defining a and b. It generates:

Output Purpose
results/tables/23-reporting-evidence.csv Unrounded numeric evidence for text and tables
results/reports/23-finding-draft.md Draft paragraph calculated from the analysis
results/reports/23-session-info.txt R and package environment information
results/reports/23-input-checksum.csv Input MD5 fingerprint for accidental-change tracking

A checksum helps identify whether file bytes changed; it does not verify data accuracy or replace access controls and provenance. Keep original inputs and analysis decisions alongside the scripts.

Pre-generated R outputs are not included or required for chapter rendering. Review the generated paragraph before publication; software cannot infer unrecorded design details or substantive limitations.

10. Use AI and check the draft

Using only the supplied evidence table and verified study notes, draft a concise methods description and results paragraph. Preserve the A-minus-B direction, units, sample sizes, interval method, and p-value. Separate observed findings from interpretation. Identify missing facts instead of inventing them. Do not turn a nonsignificant test into equivalence or an association into causation. List every numerical statement so I can check it against the exported results.

Check every number against the saved output, every claim against the design, and every reference against its actual source. Keep track of consequential AI suggestions and revisions. Follow applicable institutional or publication requirements for disclosing AI assistance.

Use synthetic data or authorized de-identified summaries when seeking drafting help. A fluent paragraph is not evidence that the underlying calculation or claim is correct.

11. Review before sharing

Ask yourself:

  1. Can the reader identify the question and independent unit?
  2. Are estimates, units, direction, and uncertainty correct?
  3. Are missingness, exclusions, and analysis changes documented?
  4. Are assumptions and diagnostic findings described accurately?
  5. Is multiplicity handled and labeled where relevant?
  6. Does the interpretation distinguish association, prediction, and causation?
  7. Can another person trace the paragraph to the inputs, script, and outputs?

Resolve discrepancies between figures, tables, and text before sharing. Preserve the exact output version used in the report.

12. Check your understanding

An AI draft changes “A minus B was -4.80 points” to “A improved scores by 4.80 points compared with B.”

Which claims changed?

Check your reasoning

The draft reverses the direction: the original estimate indicates a lower mean in A. It also introduces improvement over time and a causal effect, neither established by this independent-group comparison. Restore the signed estimate, interval, and design-appropriate wording.
